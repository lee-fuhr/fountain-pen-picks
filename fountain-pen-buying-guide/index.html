How to choose a fountain pen

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

A fountain pen buying guide for 2026 should help you choose based on nib size, filling mechanism, grip comfort, and your writing habits. Start with a medium or fine nib, a cartridge-converter pen, and a comfortable grip, then experiment from there. You don’t need to spend hundreds to get a reliable writer; many excellent entry-level pens exist from established brands.

Which nib size should a beginner choose?

A medium nib is the safest starting point because it balances smoothness and ink flow without being too wet or too scratchy. Fine nibs are great if you have small handwriting or need to write on poor-quality paper, while broad nibs show off ink shading and sheen but can feather on absorbent paper. Many people end up owning a couple of sizes once they get hooked, so don’t overthink it, medium or fine will serve you well for everyday use.

Cartridge, converter, or piston filler: which is best?

Cartridge pens are the simplest: pop in a plastic ink cartridge and you’re done. Converters let you use bottled ink with the same pen, giving you a much wider color choice, most beginners grab a piston-type converter that works like a miniature syringe. Piston-filler pens (like several from TWSBI and Pelikan) have built-in mechanisms that hold more ink and don’t need separate converters, but they’re a bit harder to clean. For your first pen, a standard cartridge-converter model gives you the best of both worlds.

Does the grip section really matter?

Absolutely. The grip is where your fingers touch the pen, so its shape, material, and thickness affect comfort during long writing sessions. Some models have a triangular molded grip (common in many school pens) that can help position your hand, while others are round and smooth. If you have a tight grip or larger hands, look for a wider, softly textured grip. Avoid glossy metal sections if your hands get sweaty, they can become slippery after a few paragraphs.

Steel vs. gold nibs: what’s the real difference?

Steel nibs are standard on most pens under a few hundred dollars. They’re stiff, durable, and write reliably with no fuss. Gold nibs (usually 14K or 18K) are softer and can offer a subtle springy feel, which some people find more comfortable for long writing sessions. Gold nibs also resist corrosion better, but the price jump is significant. For a first pen, a high-quality steel nib from a reputable brand will outperform a cheap gold nib every time.

What should you spend as a beginner?

You can find a perfectly usable fountain pen for a very modest budget, these are often plastic-bodied pens with decent steel nibs from brands like Pilot, Lamy, or Platinum. A step up into the low-to-mid price range gets you better build quality, nicer materials (metal or resin), and sometimes a converter included. There’s no need to spend more than that until you know what you like. Remember that the paper you use matters just as much as the pen.

Frequently asked questions

Is a fountain pen difficult to use compared to a ballpoint?

Not really, fountain pens require almost no pressure; you just let the pen glide. The main adjustment is holding it at a consistent angle and not pressing down hard. After a few pages it feels natural.

What ink should I start with?

Start with the ink cartridges or bottled ink made by the same brand as your pen. They’re reliable and designed to flow well in that pen’s feed. Once you’re comfortable, you can explore thousands of other colors from makers like Diamine, Waterman, and Noodler’s.

How do I clean a fountain pen?

Flush the nib and feed with cool water until it runs clear. For cartridge-converter pens, you can use a bulb syringe to force water through the section. Avoid hot water and never use alcohol, it can damage the plastic or finish.

Why does my fountain pen skip or stop writing?

Skipping usually means the nib is dry, the tines are too tight, or the ink has dried in the feed. Try flushing the pen with water, using a different ink, or gently spreading the tines with a brass shim if you’re comfortable. Sometimes it’s just paper fibers stuck in the slit.

Can I use fountain pen ink on regular notebook paper?

Yes, but not all paper works well. Cheap, thin paper can cause feathering and bleed-through. Look for paper marked “fountain pen friendly” or try brands like Rhodia, Leuchtturm1917, or Oxford. Many affordable composition notebooks also handle ink better if you choose a fine nib and fast-drying ink.

What is a nib feed and why does it matter?

The feed is the plastic or ebonite piece under the nib that channels ink from the reservoir to the tip through capillary action. A well-designed feed regulates flow and prevents flooding or starvation. Most modern feeds work fine, but cheap pens sometimes have inconsistent feeds that cause trouble.

In shortA fountain pen is a personal tool, so the “best” one is the one that feels right in your hand and makes you want to write more. Start with a reliable, affordable model from a respected brand, pair it with decent paper, and don’t be afraid to try different nib sizes and inks. The journey is half the fun.
