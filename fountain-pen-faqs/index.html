Fountain pen questions answered

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

Fountain pens use liquid ink delivered through a nib and feed via capillary action, no pressure required. Choosing your first pen comes down to nib size, ink type, and a willingness to do simple cleaning. Start with a reliable, budget-friendly model, and you’ll quickly experience why these tools have delighted writers for generations.

How does a fountain pen work?

A fountain pen holds liquid ink in a reservoir, either a disposable cartridge, a refillable converter, or a built-in piston or eye-dropper system. The ink travels down a narrow channel in the feed (a plastic or ebonite piece under the nib) and reaches the slit in the nib tip. The combination of gravity and capillary action draws ink onto the paper as you write. Unlike ballpoints, fountain pens need almost no downward pressure. The ink flows with just the weight of the pen, which reduces hand fatigue over long writing sessions. The design also means the nib can be ground to different shapes and widths, offering line variation you simply cannot get from a rollerball or gel pen.

What nib size should I choose?

Nib sizes range from extra-fine (EF) to broad (B), plus specialty grinds like stub or italic. An extra-fine nib lays down a thin line similar to a 0.3mm gel pen; it’s ideal for small handwriting and cheap, absorbent paper. A fine nib is the most common starting point, versatile for everyday note-taking. Medium and broad nibs produce thicker, wetter lines that show off ink properties like shading and sheen, but they need good paper to avoid feathering. Stub and italic nibs create crisp line variation on downstrokes vs. side strokes, popular for calligraphy. If you’re unsure, a fine or medium nib on a pen that lets you swap nibs (like a Lamy Safari or TWSBI Eco) is a safe bet. You can always go broader later.

What type of ink should I use?

Only use water-based ink made specifically for fountain pens. India ink, calligraphy dip-pen ink, or acrylic ink will clog the feed and ruin the pen. Standard fountain-pen inks from brands like Diamine, Waterman, and Herbin are reliable, easy to clean, and available in hundreds of colors. Within fountain-pen inks, the main choices are dye-based (smooth, vibrant, easy to flush) and pigment-based (water-resistant, fade-proof, but require more thorough cleaning). Shimmer inks contain tiny glitter particles and look spectacular but need a broad nib and extra rinsing. For everyday carry, a dye-based ink in a standard color (black, blue-black, or dark teal) is the most trouble-free place to start.

Is a fountain pen hard to maintain?

Fountain pens are not high-maintenance, but they do need some care. The most important rule: never let ink dry inside the pen. If you use the pen at least once a week, it will usually stay wet and ready. For long breaks, empty and rinse the pen with cool, distilled water until it runs clear. Every time you switch inks, flush the pen, a bulb syringe speeds the process. Most modern pens can go months without a deep clean if you stick to the same ink. If the nib becomes scratchy or skips, a quick flush often solves it. Avoid using hot water, alcohol, or ultrasonic cleaners unless the manufacturer approves. Simple habits keep a fountain pen writing smoothly for years.

Which fountain pen is best for beginners?

The best beginner pen is one that is durable, forgiving, and easy to clean. Models like the Lamy Safari, Pilot Metropolitan, and Platinum Preppy are consistently recommended by owners and experts because they write reliably out of the box and survive the occasional drop or missed cleaning. These pens are budget-friendly, use standard cartridges or converters, and have nibs that handle a range of inks without fuss. A pen with a snap cap and a metal grip (like the Pilot Metropolitan) gives a reassuring weight, while the Safari’s ergonomic grip helps you hold it at the correct angle. The Preppy is the cheapest but still writes well. For a step up, TWSBI Eco offers a built-in piston filler that holds a lot of ink and lets you see the reservoir. Any of these will serve admirably as a daily writer while you learn what you like.

Can left-handed people use fountain pens?

Yes, many left-handed writers use fountain pens successfully. The key is choosing the right nib and ink. A fine or extra-fine nib reduces smearing because it lays down less ink. Fast-drying inks, ones labeled as “dry” or those from brands like Pelikan, Waterman, or R&K. also help. Smooth, less absorbent paper dries more slowly, so avoid glossy or coated sheets. Some left-handers prefer nibs ground specifically for push strokes (left-foot oblique), but most find a standard fine nib works fine with a slight change in hand position, either under-writing or side-writing. The pen should be held lightly with the nib at the same angle as a right-handed writer. It takes a little practice, but the payoff is a comfortable, expressive writing tool that doesn’t force you to bear down.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my fountain pen skip or scratch?

Skipping usually means the nib isn’t getting enough ink, often due to dried ink in the feed or a baby’s bottom (over-polished tipping). Scratching often indicates misaligned tines. First, flush the pen thoroughly with water. If that doesn’t help, check the tine alignment with a loupe; a gentle adjustment or a quick visit to a nib specialist may be needed.

How often should I clean my fountain pen?

Clean the pen every time you switch ink colors or at least every 4 to 6 weeks if you leave the same ink inside. For heavily pigmented or shimmer inks, clean after each fill. Never let ink dry completely in the feed, or you may need to soak the nib unit overnight.

Can I use fountain pen ink in a dip pen?

Yes, but fountain pen ink is thinner than dip-pen ink, so it may run off the nib too quickly or feather on certain papers. It works fine for practice or sketching if you’re gentle. For serious calligraphy with a dip nib, use a dedicated dip-pen ink or gum arabic to thicken the fountain pen ink slightly.

What is the difference between a converter and a cartridge?

A cartridge is a small plastic tube of ink that snaps into the pen, convenient and disposable. A converter is a refillable piston mechanism that fits into the same space, letting you draw ink from a bottle. Converters reduce plastic waste and give you access to hundreds of bottled ink colors. Many pens accept both, so you can start with cartridges and switch to a converter later.

How do I hold a fountain pen correctly?

Hold the pen with a light, relaxed grip, no more pressure than resting your finger on a table. The nib should face upward (the slit on top). Let the pen glide under its own weight; you should not press down. A standard tripod grip works well, but some people prefer an overhand or even a modified grasp. The nib will write at almost any angle, but for best flow keep the nib’s tipping fully in contact with the paper.

Why does my pen leak?

Leaks are usually caused by air getting into the ink reservoir. Common culprits: the pen is stored nib-down (ink drips into the cap), sudden temperature changes (warm hand expands air inside), or a loose cartridge/converter. Always store the pen horizontally or nib-up. If the leak persists, check for a cracked feed or nib housing. A few drops of silicone grease on threads can seal piston or converter issues.

In shortFountain pens reward a little patience with a writing experience that feels effortless and personal. Start with a well-reviewed model in a nib size that suits your hand, pair it with a reliable water-based ink, and make gentle cleaning part of your routine. You don’t need a big budget or deep knowledge, just a willingness to enjoy the process. Once you find that first combination that writes like butter, you’ll understand why so many people never go back.
